LANGUAGE · HIERARCHICAL MODELS · 2026

What’s in a tree?

Language has structure across many scales. How can we vary that structure and measure its effect on learning? We introduce tree-grams: trainable language models with separate controls for context length and capacity.

A project with Francesco Cagnetta, Darshil Doshi, Victor V. Albert, Maissam Barkeshli, Andrey Gromov.

Two controls on a language model

A conventional n-gram model predicts a token from the previous n−1n-1n−1 tokens. Storing every possible context becomes expensive as the window grows.

Tree-grams build the conditional distribution p(xn∣x1:n−1)p(x_n \mid x_{1:n-1})p(xn​∣x1:n−1​) through a hierarchy of small tensor operations. The horizon nnn sets the context window. The rank ddd controls capacity. These controls let us generate different approximations of the same corpus.

Controlled data as an experiment

We train the generators on text, then use their samples to study learning. On TinyStories, tuning narrow transformers on simpler generated data improves learning-rate transfer under maximal update parametrization (μP). This advantage does not hold for FineWeb-Edu.

Continued training on a new distribution also depends on the target’s entropy and correlation range. Allowing tree-grams to select among topologies produces sparse routing: up to 95% of candidate topologies can be pruned with little performance change in the tested setting.

What the comparison tells us

Tree-grams outperform the tested traditional n-gram baselines. A gap to deep transformers remains at longer contexts. Their purpose is to make language a controlled experimental setting.

A few useful terms

  • Horizon. The maximal window of nnn tokens, including the token to predict.
  • Rank. The capacity parameter ddd of the tree-gram.
  • Autoregressive model. A model that generates a sequence one token at a time, conditioned on earlier tokens.
